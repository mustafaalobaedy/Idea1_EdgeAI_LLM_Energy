# Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are being moved from the datacenter to the edge. When a model is run on the device that collects the data, private text is kept local. Network latency is removed, and the service stays available without a connection [@shi2016edge; @zhou2019edge]. This move has been made practical by three developments. Small language models (SLMs) with 0.5 to 8 billion parameters have been released [@meta2024llama32; @gemma2024gemma2; @qwen2024qwen25; @yang2025qwen3]. Weight quantization to 4 bits has been shown to keep most of the accuracy [@frantar2023gptq; @lin2024awq; @dettmers2023case]. The llama.cpp engine has made such models easy to serve on consumer hardware [@gerganov2023llamacpp]. A single-board computer such as the NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin can now serve a 7B-parameter model from a power budget of a few tens of watts. Similar edge platforms have been used by the author for distributed computation on Raspberry Pi clusters [@alobaedy2022raspberry] and for workload allocation in body area networks [@alahakoon2024edge].

On such a device, energy is not an afterthought. The device may be run from a battery, a solar panel, or a fixed thermal envelope. Every generated token draws measurable current from a shared power rail. Several *power modes* are also offered by the device. Each mode caps the CPU and GPU frequencies, and some modes switch off GPU cores, to meet a budget of 15, 30, or 40 W. These choices interact. In a lower power mode, less power is drawn, but the run is made longer. With a smaller or more heavily quantized model, each query is answered faster, but fewer questions may be answered correctly. With a reasoning model, more questions may be answered correctly, but thousands of tokens may be spent on an easy problem [@chen2025overthinking; @sui2025stop]. The question that follows is simple. Which combination gives the most *useful work* for a fixed amount of energy?

A different question is answered by most edge measurement studies. Tokens per second, tokens per joule, or perplexity are reported [@seymour2025large; @giedra2026benchmarking; @arya2025understanding; @taherin2026hydra]. By these metrics, it is described how fast a model writes, not whether what is written is right. A wrong answer that is produced quickly is cheap per token but worthless per task. In accuracy-oriented quantization studies, quality per bit is reported on datacenter GPUs, and energy on real edge hardware is not measured [@li2024evaluating; @jin2024comprehensive]. Accuracy and energy have been combined on edge devices in only a few works. In those works, a CPU-only board was used [@husom2025sustainable], devices rather than power modes were compared [@islam2025characterizing], or the analysis was stopped at an accuracy–latency frontier [@kubwimana2025edgereasoning]. To the best of the author's knowledge, the energy cost of a *correct answer* has not been measured on a single edge GPU. In particular, model size, numerical precision, power mode, and reasoning effort have not been varied together.

This gap is filled in the present paper with one controlled measurement campaign on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin 32 GB. Twenty-seven model configurations from four open-weight families (Qwen2.5, Llama 3.2, Gemma 2, and Qwen3) were served through Ollama [@ollama2023ollama]. Quantization levels from Q3_K_M to FP16 were covered. The two stock power modes that can be switched at run time, MAXN and 40 W, were swept. The same fixed subsets of three benchmarks were answered by every configuration. The benchmarks were chosen to stress different inference regimes: ARC-Challenge [@clark2018think], BoolQ [@clark2019boolq], and GSM8K [@cobbe2021training]. Module power was sampled at 50 Hz from the on-board current sensors and integrated over every request, so that each question was given its own measured energy. The primary metric is **joules per correct answer (JPCA)**, the expected energy that must be spent to obtain one correct answer. Wrong answers are charged to the energy bill by this metric. Cheap-but-wrong and expensive-but-accurate configurations are therefore placed on the same scale.

Five research questions are asked:

- BULLET: **RQ1 (Size and precision).** How are accuracy, energy, and latency traded against each other by model size and quantization level, and which configurations are Pareto-optimal on each task?
- BULLET: **RQ2 (Power modes).** Is energy per correct answer saved by a lower power budget? Or is the lower power draw canceled by the longer run time, as predicted for processors by the race-to-idle literature [@lesueur2010dynamic; @awan2014race]?
- BULLET: **RQ3 (Quantization ladder).** With architecture and training held fixed, where on the ladder from 3-bit to 16-bit weights does additional precision stop paying for itself?
- BULLET: **RQ4 (Thinking).** Within one hybrid model, how much energy is spent by an explicit reasoning phase per extra correct answer?
- BULLET: **RQ5 (Cascades).** Can every single model be beaten on measured energy at equal accuracy by a training-free combination of small and large models?

The contributions of this paper are as follows.

- BULLET: **An accuracy-normalized energy benchmark for edge LLM inference.** Per-question energy, latency, power, temperature, and model-output logs are released for 27 configurations, two power modes, and three tasks. Full-resolution power traces are included. The campaign can be reproduced with one script.
- BULLET: **A power-mode sweep with fixed outputs.** Because decoding is greedy, the model answers are not changed by the power mode. This property is verified empirically, and the pure energy and latency effect of the 40 W budget relative to MAXN is thereby isolated.
- BULLET: **A six-level quantization ladder on one model.** Qwen2.5-3B is measured at Q3_K_M, Q4_K_M, Q5_K_M, Q6_K, Q8_0, and FP16. The effect of numerical precision is thereby separated from the effect of architecture.
- BULLET: **A per-joule price for reasoning.** Thinking is switched on and off in Qwen3 1.7B, 4B, and 8B on the same items. The marginal energy per additional correct answer is reported.
- BULLET: **An energy-aware agreement cascade.** Two small models answer first, and a larger model is called only when they disagree. The cascade is evaluated on measured per-item energies with a held-out split, together with an oracle-router upper bound.
- BULLET: **A measurement protocol for shared edge devices.** An idle-machine guard and test–retest checks are documented, and the need for them is shown from a pilot run.

All energies are module-level values read from the on-board INA3221 sensors. Absolute joule values therefore carry the calibration uncertainty of the sensor [@shalavi2023accurate]. The conclusions rest on ratios and rankings between configurations measured with the same sensors on the same device, where a consistent sensor bias is canceled.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Related work on energy accounting, efficient on-device models, edge measurement studies, and cascades is reviewed in the next section. The hardware, measurement method, models, tasks, metrics, and statistical analysis are then described in the Materials & Methods section. The results are reported for each research question in the Results section. Practical guidance, limitations, and threats to validity are given in the Discussion section. The paper is closed by the Conclusions section.
